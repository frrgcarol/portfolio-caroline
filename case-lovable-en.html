Journey 01 · Tempo · Product · AI · Ops

No one was going to prioritize this feature. I solved it myself.

How a low-priority roadmap item — but a high-drain one for the ops team — became an interactive product I built myself, without waiting for a slot in the engineering queue.

My role
PM, designer, and builder — solo
Tool

Context

How the ops team handled requests with options

Tempo is a Brazilian platform that organizes clients' day-to-day finances and tasks — payments, purchases, quotes, scheduling — through human ops staff working over WhatsApp, backed by AI. When a request had multiple options to choose from (products, quotes, flights), the ops person would paste the product link into a template feature, which generated a PDF with the options for the client to decide.

When the link came from a site without scraping protection — the technique of automatically pulling info from a page, like price, image, and description, without manually copying it all — it worked out of the box. The problem started when the site blocked that kind of extraction.

The problem

A PDF that almost no one could edit quickly

When the site blocked scraping — which happened often — the ops person had to open a generic PDF editor and build the template from scratch: create each text box, paste the info, pick a font, position everything on the page. If the product text ran long, the layout broke and they had to rearrange everything by hand again. A simple template could take over 30 minutes just on that manual formatting work.

That time carried a cost beyond the annoyance: it was time the ops person wasn't spending on what actually mattered to the client — finding the right product, comparing options, understanding what they needed — because they were stuck formatting text in a PDF editor.

The ops team complained a lot, especially about the manual cases. But looking at the roadmap, this feature wasn't a priority: improving it in its existing format demanded significant engineering effort for something that moved none of the metrics the team tracked closely.

Low impact on the metrics, high drain on operations — the kind of problem that stays invisible in any metric-based prioritization, but that blocks people every single day.
What I decided to do

If it wasn't going into the queue, I'd build it outside of it

As Tempo's sole PM, owning the product end-to-end, I already design my own screens in Figma and Lovable (an AI product-building tool, no code required). So instead of fighting for a slot in an engineering queue for an incremental improvement to an old system, I decided to scrap the existing feature and build a new one from scratch on my own.

The only dependency on the engineering team was a small one: asking a dev to make it so that clicking the feature icon in the Cockpit (the internal panel where the ops team works) redirected straight to my app.

What I built

From "paste a link and hope" to a real template builder

The new version handled both the easy path and the hard one — and went beyond what the old feature could do:

01

Automatic layout

The app repositioned and resized everything on its own, even with longer text. The ops person just filled in the data — no text boxes, fonts, or manual layout wrangling.

02

Link or fast manual entry

Pasting the link still worked when the site allowed it. When it didn't, manual entry became fast — not a 30-minute-plus process.

03

Multiple photos per product

Just paste each image URL — the ops person could build a full carousel in almost no time.

04

Custom fields

For specific quotes ("does this car wash offer X or not?"), you could add a field just for that question — flexibility the old PDF never had.

05

CSV import, as a table

For cases like flight options, you could paste a CSV and generate the template as a table right away — faster to scan than cards.

And the end result was no longer a static PDF

It became an interactive link: the client picked their preferred option right there, and the choice flowed straight back to Tempo's WhatsApp — the ops person already saw the decision without needing to ask again.

Before and after

What actually changed for daily users

Before
  • Static PDF, no client interaction
  • Layout built by hand: text box, font, position
  • Rearrange everything again if the text didn't fit
  • Over 30 minutes per template
  • Client's choice didn't flow back — someone had to check and relay it
  • Editing after the fact meant redoing most of the manual work
  • No custom fields
  • No table option for long lists
After
  • Interactive link — client chooses directly
  • Layout repositions and resizes on its own
  • Fast manual entry when needed
  • Template ready in under 3 minutes
  • Client's choice flows back to the ops WhatsApp automatically
  • Easy to edit afterward, if something needed adjusting
  • Custom fields per request
  • CSV table mode for lists (e.g. flights)
Impact

A small problem on paper, big at scale

In isolation, saving time on one template seems minor. But each of the 30 ops staff generated an average of 2 templates a day — around 300 templates a week across the whole operation. Applying the before-and-after time to that volume gives a real sense of the problem's size:

300 templates/week × 30 min (before) ≈ 150h/week
300 templates/week × 3 min (after) ≈ 15h/week

Beyond the time saved, the ops team's recurring complaints about this feature simply stopped. And when the occasional bug appeared after launch, I went into the code myself, fixed it, and shipped the change.

What I'd do differently

Partial metrics — I have the volume, not the conversion

I know how many templates get created, but I never instrumented more robust metrics: how many clients actually engaged with the link before deciding, how many purchases it generated, and I don't have a direct before-and-after comparison. What I have most strongly is qualitative — the ops team's complaints about this feature simply vanished, and I got several unprompted shout-outs in the company's internal channels because of it. Next time, I'd design that tracking from day one, so I could back up the impact with numbers, not just perception.
