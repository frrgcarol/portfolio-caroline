Journey 03 · Qulture.Rocks · Discovery · Data · HR Tech

The churn study that became a product hypothesis

How a statistical test revealed why clients were canceling, and turned into a product bet to raise the platform's perceived value year-round — not just during performance review season.

My role
Discovery, statistical analysis, and product hypothesis
Method
Chi-square test
Timeframe
Turn of 2024
Result
Guided the next year's product bet
Context

A product sold for one moment of the year

Qulture.Rocks is a performance management and organizational culture platform. Our best-selling product was the Performance Review (AVD) — but it only ran once or twice a year. Outside those windows, a good chunk of clients had no strong reason to keep using the platform day to day.

It was also a period of harsher financial pressure due to the pandemic — companies were cutting anything considered "nice to have," and we needed to make sure the platform wasn't seen that way.

The problem

Churn rising, with no clear cause mapped

At the turn of 2024, the company's churn was climbing, and no one had a structured explanation — just loose guesses. I decided to run Qulture.Rocks' first churn study to understand, with real data, what was going on.

The analysis

What the chi-square test revealed

I ran a chi-square test across several client behavior variables. It's a statistical method that checks whether there's an association between two categorical variables — for example, between a client's usage pattern (Performance Review only vs. multiple products) and whether they canceled. It compares what was observed against what you'd expect if there were no relationship between the variables, to tell whether the difference is real (statistically significant) or just a coincidence in the data we happened to have.

The result showed a clear pattern: clients who used only the Performance Review product — our best-seller — canceled far more easily than clients who also used other products on the platform. It made sense: if the review only happens once or twice a year, the client didn't necessarily want to keep paying for the platform the rest of the year.

Lack of engagement outside the review window led to a lack of perceived value — and a lack of perceived value led to the urge to cancel.
The hypothesis

If the problem was engagement, which product could fix it?

Based on the analysis, I formed the hypothesis that increasing platform usage in the months outside the review window could raise perceived value and reduce churn. The question was: which product had real potential to sustain that usage year-round?

My bet was the PDI (Individual Development Plan) — because it was already directly connected to the Performance Review (every review could generate a PDI), and unlike the review, it made sense to use the PDI year-round, tracking an employee's development continuously.

Another pattern in the data

HR engagement was also product engagement

The same statistical analysis surfaced something else important: the companies most engaged with the platform were exactly the ones where the HR person in charge kept in constant touch with us — frequent meetings, active questions, close follow-up.

I passed that insight to the Customer Success team, who built a plan to pull in more check-in meetings with less-engaged companies, helping them get more day-to-day value out of the platform.

What I did in the product

Removing friction for people already using the PDI

I started working on the PDI with the goal of cutting usage friction as much as possible. Two main fronts:

01

Notifications at every step

I started notifying users at every step of the PDI, keeping the development plan top of mind throughout the year, not just during review season.

02

Filling it out from tools already in use

The highest-potential change: giving people the option to fill out the PDI directly from tools they already used at work, like Slack — removing the friction of having to open yet another separate platform.

What I'd do differently

I didn't stay long enough to see churn move

I left the company a few months after starting this work, and churn isn't a metric that responds quickly — the real effect of an engagement change only shows up months later, at the next renewal cycle. So I don't have the final number on how much churn actually dropped because of these changes.

If I had stayed, the right approach would've been cohort tracking: split companies between those who started engaging more with the PDI — measured by fill-out frequency, notification engagement, and adoption of the Slack fill-out option — and those who kept the previous pattern. I'd track usage indicators month over month as a leading signal, since churn is a slow-responding metric that only gets confirmed at the next renewal cycle, which could take up to a year. And I'd cross that with the CS team, checking whether the more-engaged group actually renewed more or signaled less intent to cancel in check-in conversations — only that comparison would let me back up the impact with data, not just perception.
