Journey 02 · Tempo · Product · Discovery · Payments

Devs used to stop coding to investigate payment errors. Now that time goes into building.

How an outdated notification screen became an operations hub — giving the ops team independence to resolve payment errors on their own, plus WhatsApp automation to keep clients informed and the process moving.

Context

How Tempo decides between solving it alone or calling in a human

Tempo works like a concierge over WhatsApp: the client asks for something, and an AI triages it as either a payment or a task. If it's a payment, the AI tries to execute it on its own. If it's a task, the AI asks for whatever info is missing — following a checklist it identifies itself based on the task type — until it has enough to create the task on a kanban board for a human ops person to handle.

To keep track of all this, the ops team had a notifications screen in the Cockpit (the internal panel), split into three columns: unread conversations, task notifications, and payments due or overdue in the last 3 days — plus reminders clients could add to their calendar.

The problem

A screen that went stale, missing exactly the most critical information

This notification screen only made sense for payments sent manually. As we automated more sending, it became less and less useful — and a completed payment didn't automatically drop off the list, which already made tracking harder.

But the bigger problem was different: when a payment failed, the information didn't show up anywhere accessible. The ops person only found out if the client complained. Then they had to hunt down that specific payment inside that client's screen, with zero translation of the technical error, and almost always had to call a dev to dig into what happened in the code.

This created client dissatisfaction, overloaded the devs — who stopped building to investigate errors — and left the ops team constantly lost. And every stuck error also meant stuck money: the faster it got resolved, the faster that value went back into circulation on the platform.
Discovery

The real problem wasn't the notification — it was switching screens

Part of this diagnosis came from ongoing discovery: I read the ops team's tickets daily and had already been hearing complaints over time. But the real breakthrough came from sitting beside them and watching the actual work — a structured process of about two weeks, covering observation, mapping errors with the financial institution, writing the PRD, and validating with the ops team themselves.

The ops team worked on a single monitor, usually a small one. There was no notifications header visible on every screen of the platform — so, to resolve a payment, they had to leave the Home screen, open the Payments screen for that specific client, resolve it, and go back. That meant opening too many tabs and constantly losing their place mid-process.

The obvious first solution would be a global notifications header — but technically it was too expensive to build from scratch at that point. So the challenge became something else: how to adapt the existing screen to solve the real problem.

The problem wasn't "we need a better notification." It was "they can't afford to switch screens to solve anything."
The turning point

If it couldn't be a global header, it became an operations hub

Instead of just improving the existing notification, I decided to transform the whole screen's concept: from a screen of alerts, into an operations screen — where the ops person could see and resolve everything without ever needing to leave it.

What I built

A layout that adapts to what the ops person is doing

01

Two resizable columns

Conversations on one side, notifications on the other (with tabs for tasks, payments, and reminders). Clicking a conversation minimizes notifications; clicking a notification minimizes the conversation.

02

Resolution without switching screens

Clicking a conversation opened it right there, without leaving for the client's screen. Clicking a payment made every resolution action available instantly.

03

28 payment errors mapped

I went through the financial institution's (Celcoin) documentation and mapped every possible error across Pix, Boleto, and TED, with the right action for each — resend, "un-expire," edit missing info, and more.

04

Technical error translated into a human action

The notification's name already explained the problem (e.g. "Payment missing information"), with amount, client, and date visible — and the instructions on how to resolve it right below.

The PRD as a bridge to engineering

Unlike the templates case, here I didn't build the final version myself — it needed engineering to integrate with real payment data. So I wrote a table in the PRD with each of the 28 errors: title, explanation, and the available action for each case. To remove any ambiguity, I also replicated every error inside a full Lovable prototype, so the devs could see exactly how each screen should behave before building it.

Closing the loop with AI

Automating what never needed a human in the first place

After the screen shipped, I automated whatever made sense to resolve directly on WhatsApp, without relying on the ops team. When a payment was one day out from being sent and some info was missing, the AI would chase the client automatically. If they didn't reply within a few hours, the AI followed up again — and only then generated the notification for the ops person to push harder.

When an error happened, the AI would proactively notify the client, with a suggested action: "this payment couldn't go through because it was outside boleto processing hours — want to schedule it for the next business slot?" That left the human ops person with only what the AI genuinely couldn't resolve on its own — without flooding their queue with cases the AI had already handled.

Before and after

From "call a dev" to "the ops person solves it alone"

Before
  • Error only discovered when the client complained
  • No translation of the technical error
  • Constant dependency on a dev to investigate
  • Notifications and resolution on separate screens
  • Screen useful only for manual payments
After
  • Error appears instantly, with full context
  • Technical error translated into a clear action
  • Ops person resolves it alone, no dev needed
  • Notification and resolution on the same screen
  • AI resolves part of the cases directly on WhatsApp
Impact

From firefighting to building the product

28

1 month
From discovery with the ops team to screen launch

Error-investigation requests to devs, after launch

The ops team no longer depended on engineering to understand what happened to a payment — and gained full control to resolve it, right on the screen. The time devs used to spend digging through code for errors went back to where it belonged: building and improving the rest of the product.

What I'd do differently

The launch order created rework that could've been avoided

I launched the operations screen first, and only later the AI piece that detects errors and acts directly on WhatsApp. We did it in that order because the lack of payment visibility was already causing real financial loss, so centralizing that information was urgent. But in practice, the screen took about a month from discovery to launch, counting dev time — and it created some rework: when I launched the WhatsApp AI automation, which the devs built in about a week, I realized it already resolved some error types 100% on its own, and I had to delete notifications I'd just built.

Next time, I'd flip the order: first map and automate everything the AI could already resolve on its own — which took a fraction of the time — and only then design the screen, covering exactly what was left for the ops person. It would've been faster and with zero rework.
